About

Kyle Lunsford

Computer Science student at UNC Charlotte with a concentration in Data Science. I like taking a messy problem, splitting it into steps, and shipping something people can actually use — two of my apps are live on the App Store.

LinkedIn View work

I got into programming because I liked the idea that you could think of something on a Tuesday and have people using it by the weekend. That is still the part I enjoy most. Most of what I build starts as a small annoyance — someone losing track of what is in their pantry, a church that cannot update its own app — and turns into a project I keep pushing until it is genuinely usable rather than just a demo.

My concentration is Data Science, so a lot of my coursework is Python: cleaning messy data, building models, and being honest about what the numbers actually mean. My favorite project so far is a dementia classifier trained on about 44,000 brain MRI slices. Dementia has touched my family, and teaching myself convolutional neural networks to push that project past the course requirements was the moment the material stopped feeling like homework.

The other half of my time goes to iOS. I taught myself SwiftUI, shipped Item Tracking to the App Store, and then built the Meadow Grove Church app end to end — SwiftUI client, FastAPI backend, Postgres, and push notifications — so church leaders could publish their own content without waiting on me for a release. Shipping to real users taught me more about edge cases, privacy, and release process than any assignment could.

How I work

Habits that show up in every project here.

Ship, then sharpen

I get a rough version working early so I can react to something real. Item Tracking is on version 1.2.1 because shipping surfaced problems I would never have guessed on paper.

Measure honestly

A good number that hides a flaw is worse than a mediocre one. On the MRI project I reported macro-F1 instead of accuracy and documented the patient-level leakage in the dataset.

Build for the person using it

Most of my design decisions come from watching someone get stuck. Offline-first storage, plain language, and no account required unless the feature truly needs one.
